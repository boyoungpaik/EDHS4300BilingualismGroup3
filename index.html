Has bilingualism become obsolete because of machine translation?

Translation apps are free, fast and on almost every phone. This site looks at what they do well, where they still fall short, and why we think knowing a second language matters as much as ever.

01

Words with more than one meaning

Section lead: Sanjay Lakshmanan

Many words carry several meanings. In Spanish, seguro can mean "safe," "sure" or "insurance." Banco can be a bank or a bench. Esposas can be wives or handcuffs.

People figure out the right meaning almost instantly because they know the situation, the speaker and what was said a moment ago. A translation app only has the words on the screen, so it has to guess from the words around them. Modern apps look at whole sentences, which helps a lot, but mistakes still happen when a phrase is short or the context is missing.

Idioms and slang are even harder. A literal translation of "break a leg" sounds like a threat, and "no cap" has nothing to do with hats.

02

Tone and body language

Section lead: Hannah Kim

Machine translation has two big blind spots. It has trouble with tone, pitch and emotion, and it has trouble reading body language in its cultural context.

Tone changes meaning

Take the sentence "I'm fine." Said warmly, it means things really are fine. Said sarcastically or hesitantly, it can mean the opposite. A machine translates the words literally, so the underlying meaning disappears.

Same words, different meaning

Choose a tone. The machine output never changes.

Spanish output"Estoy bien."

Word stress works the same way. In "She didn't take your money," stressing a different word changes what the speaker is implying. Stressing "she" suggests someone else took it. Text-based translation drops that extra layer of meaning.

Gestures depend on culture

Head movements look universal, but they aren't. In the United States, nodding up and down means "yes" and shaking side to side means "no." In Bulgaria, the meanings are reversed. Even with advanced computer vision, a translation system could misread these gestures unless it accounts for cultural context.

TakeawayTranslation takes more than recognizing words and gestures. It takes social and cultural context.
03

Dialects and training data

Section lead: Bo Paik

This section looks at how translation apps learn languages, and why that process leaves some speakers behind.

Apps learn "standard" language

Machine translators like Google Translate learn from large collections of parallel text that humans have already translated. Most of that material reflects the "standard" variety of a language. As a result, apps may not be tuned to the vocabulary, grammar and pragmatics that differ from one dialect to another. Spanish in Spain, Mexico and the Caribbean differs in vocabulary, idioms and sometimes grammar, and a word that is ordinary in one country can sound awkward or even offensive in another.

Apps depend on bilingual people

Translation apps can't replace bilingual knowledge because they are built from it. Bilingual people produce the translated texts the systems learn from, and bilingual evaluators judge whether the output is accurate. An app's accuracy and biases come from its training data.

This matters because language keeps changing. New words come in, old ones fade, and meanings shift. An app can only stay current if it keeps learning from people who use both languages in real life. If bilingual speakers disappeared, translations would slowly drift away from how people actually talk, with nobody left to catch the mistakes.

Machine translation and linguistic hierarchy

Many minority languages are poorly represented in training data because they have fewer speakers and less presence in media, so translation is less reliable for them. This is an equity issue. Whose dialects and languages are prioritized in training data decides which communities get good translations and which get poor ones, or none at all.

How much of the world's linguistic diversity does Google Translate cover?

That's about 3.5%. Because Google's count includes varieties of the same language, the share of distinct languages is even smaller.

For minority language communities, bilingual speakers are what keep the language alive.

TakeawayMachine translation can help with basic travel needs, but it is built from bilingual knowledge and can't make bilingualism obsolete.
04

Medical and travel situations

Section lead: Harrison Stone

Machine translation falls short in exactly the situations where accuracy and human understanding matter most.

Traveling

Passing a phone back and forth is slow and breaks up the conversation. That becomes a real problem when time is short, like negotiating with a taxi driver, rebooking a missed flight or asking for directions in a hurry. Noisy streets, weak signals and dead batteries also make apps unreliable. Speaking even a little of the local language builds a kind of trust that a screen can't.

Hospitals and courts

In medical and legal settings the stakes are much higher. A mistranslated allergy, medication dose or consent form can cause real harm, which is why hospitals and courts use certified interpreters instead of consumer apps.

A real example from an emergency department

From a 2019 UCSF study of Google Translate and emergency discharge instructions (Khoong et al., 2019)

Doctor wrote"Hold the kidney medicine."
MeantStop taking it for now.
TranslationSpanish: "keep the medication." Chinese: "keep taking" the medication.

The researchers rated this error as potentially life-threatening. Overall, Google Translate was 92% accurate for Spanish and 81% for Chinese. That's better than many clinicians expected, but a few errors still had the potential to cause serious harm.

Courts have run into the same problem. In one US case, a police officer used Google Translate to ask a Spanish-speaking driver for permission to search her car, and the court found the app wasn't reliable enough to show that she had really consented (Ramirez, 2024).

When the patient can't use the app

Even a perfect app is of little use to someone who has had a stroke or an injury. A patient who is in pain, confused, or unable to speak clearly or use their hands can't operate it, and emergencies leave no time to type, wait and re-check. A bilingual doctor, nurse or interpreter can talk with that patient directly. Human interpreters also adapt to the person. They adjust for accents and slang, notice confusion or fear, and rephrase to make sure they were understood.

TakeawayIn high-stakes settings, machine translation works best as a supplement to bilingual professionals, not a replacement.
05

Sign language and bilingual accessibility

Section lead: Tammy Yen

Bilingualism isn't limited to spoken languages. Someone who uses both American Sign Language (ASL) and English is bilingual. ASL is an independent language with its own grammar and sentence structure. Instead of sound, it uses handshape, movement, facial expression and physical space to carry meaning.

Facial expressions in ASL are part of the grammar. For example, raised eyebrows mark a yes/no question, and lowered, furrowed eyebrows mark a "wh-" question like who, what or where. An ASL translation app would have to do much more than recognize hand signs.

Talking directly in ASL

  • Both people simply look at each other and sign
  • Facial grammar and space are understood naturally
  • No device, signal or battery needed
  • Communication is direct and independent

AI-assisted translation

  • A camera must continuously capture hands, face and body
  • Everything has to be interpreted together in real time
  • Both people position themselves around a screen
  • Could help close gaps where no interpreter is available

AI could improve accessibility. But even if it someday translates ASL accurately in real time, relying on cameras and screens would make everyday conversation less natural than signing with someone who knows ASL. English captions also aren't always an equal substitute for ASL interpretation, especially for people whose primary language is ASL.

06

Language and identity

Section lead: Sophia Lee

Language isn't just a way to communicate. It's tied to culture, to meaning, and to who we are. Tools for crossing language barriers have come a long way, from the printing press to Google Translate to ChatGPT, and today word-for-word translation is nearly instant. When we visit a country where we don't speak the language, we're no longer completely lost.

Whether that makes bilingualism obsolete raises bigger questions. Why does learning a language matter? What does it say about your identity? And why should we keep bilingualism alive?

Different languages, different selves

Many bilingual people say they don't feel like exactly the same person in each language. A language comes with its own jokes, expressions and ways of showing affection that don't carry over well. A thought that feels natural in Mandarin or Korean can sound strange, or less like yourself, when translated directly into English. An app might translate what you say to your family accurately enough, but putting a phone between you changes the conversation.

Bilingual speakers also create things machines can't. Korean American speakers often code-mix Korean and English ("Konglish"), switching to whichever language best captures a feeling or idea. They use it differently with parents than with friends, and the surprise of an unexpected mixed phrase is often part of the humor. A translator built to convert language A into language B has no way to produce this kind of mixing.

"Afrikaans is the language of my heart."
Research participant quoted in Maurice (2026)
TakeawayMachine translation can carry your words, but it can't carry who you are when you speak them.
07

Arguments for and against bilingualism becoming obsolete

Section lead: Louisa Mulligan

This section brings the ideas from the other sections together and weighs both sides.

Arguments for "obsolete"

  • Translation apps are free, fast and on almost every phone
  • AI keeps making them more accurate
  • For everyday tasks like reading a menu or asking for directions, many people may never need a second language
  • Humans have always handed mental work to tools, like writing and calculators (Maurice, 2026)
  • Demand for human translators is falling. In one survey of European translators, 79% saw AI as a threat to their jobs (Oltermann, 2026)

Arguments against

  • Apps still struggle with double meanings, idioms, tone and cultural context (Sections 1–2)
  • They are trained mostly on "standard" languages and leave minority languages behind (Section 3)
  • They can be unreliable in medical, legal and emergency situations (Section 4)
  • They can't replace direct communication in ASL (Section 5)
  • They can't replace the identity and connection that come from speaking someone's language (Section 6)

Language researchers writing in The Conversation make a similar point. AI is good at the literal side of language but misses social meaning, humor and register, especially in less-represented languages. It can support language learning by personalizing practice and giving feedback, but it can't do the cognitive and cultural work of learning a language for you (Maurice, 2026).

Our conclusion

Machine translation is a useful tool, and it may make a second language less necessary for quick, everyday tasks. It doesn't make bilingualism less valuable. Translation supports bilingualism rather than replacing it, and translation systems themselves depend on bilingual people to exist and stay accurate.
